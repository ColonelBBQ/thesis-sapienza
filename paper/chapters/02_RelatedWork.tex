\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{The AI-CRAS Baseline: Cloud Requirement Classification}

The AI-CRAS framework \cite{casalicchio2024aicras} proposes an AI-driven methodology for automating the analysis and specification of cloud computing requirements expressed in natural language. It addresses a recognised gap in the literature: while substantial research exists on NLP-assisted requirements engineering for generic software systems and for security requirements, little work targets the cloud domain specifically, where requirements span a heterogeneous mix of functional and non-functional concerns across IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS service models.

The methodology is structured into six phases: definition of a cloud service ontology, fine-tuning of a BERT-based classifier, automatic extraction of key sentences from requirement documents, classification of sentences as requirement or non-requirement, mapping of requirements to cloud services using the ontology, and automatic production of a structured specification document. The cloud service ontology organises requirements hierarchically into six main categories — Compute, Data Handling, Network, Security \& Compliance, Management \& Monitoring, and Cloud Service Essentials — each comprising three to five subcategories and a total of 68 cloud features at the lowest level.

The dataset was constructed from public Request for Proposal (RFP) documents and seven synthetically generated documents produced using GPT-4 to enrich the training data. Each of the resulting 2,164 sentences was independently labelled by two annotators with one-hot encoded assignments to the six ontology categories; disagreements led to exclusion of the disputed sample. Requirements account for 68.1\% of the entries.

The classification model fine-tunes BERT\textsubscript{BASE-UNCASED} with a dropout layer (rate 0.3) and a linear output layer mapping the 768-dimensional pooled representation to six binary logits, trained with binary cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer. The hyperparameter configuration uses a maximum sequence length of 300 tokens, batch size of 16, learning rate of $3\times10^{-5}$, weight decay of $10^{-6}$, and a classification threshold of 0.20. On an 85/15 train-validation split, the model achieved a binary recall of 0.96 and an F1-score of 0.92, and a multi-label macro-averaged F1-score of 0.76, with a micro- and macro-averaged ROC-AUC of 0.96.

These results establish AI-CRAS as the baseline for automated cloud requirement classification and for the experiments in this thesis. The framework, however, operates under an implicit assumption that its input sentences are clear and well-formed — an assumption that rarely holds in real-world procurement documents, where requirements are routinely vague, structurally ambiguous, or context-dependent. Whether such ambiguity degrades classification performance is the question that motivates the remainder of this review.

\section{Ambiguity in Natural Language Requirements}

If ambiguity matters for classification, it is necessary to define what it is and what forms it takes. The difficulty of poorly specified requirements is not simply a matter of coverage: a requirement can be correctly scoped and still be ambiguous — open to more than one valid interpretation. An ambiguous requirement may be correctly identified \textit{as} a requirement by a classifier while still being fundamentally misunderstood by the system that acts on it. Bano \cite{bano2015ambiguity} defines ambiguity in requirements engineering as ``having multiple interpretations despite the reader's knowledge of the RE context'', and identifies it as more intractable than other defects such as incompleteness.

Bano's work also provides the structured taxonomy — building on Berry's widely adopted classification — that this thesis uses to annotate and analyse ambiguous sentences:

\begin{quote}
Lexical ambiguity of a word arises when a word can have several different meanings. Syntactic ambiguity or structural ambiguity in a sentence arises when the sentence can be parsed in more than one way, resulting in more than one grammatical structure where each provides different meanings. Semantic ambiguity arises in a sentence when predicate logic of a sentence can lead to multiple interpretations, although there is no lexical, syntactic, or structural ambiguity. Language errors ambiguity is a grammatically wrong construction that is nevertheless understood and possibly in different ways due to the error. Pragmatic ambiguity is concerned with the relationship between the interpretations of a sentence and its context. \cite{bano2015ambiguity}
\end{quote}

These five categories — lexical, syntactic, semantic, language-error, and pragmatic ambiguity — are not rare edge cases. Bano's mapping study of the RE literature found that 81\% of empirical work on requirements ambiguity focuses on detection alone, with very little attention to resolution. This thesis directly addresses that gap: rather than detecting ambiguity, it tests whether automated resolution — rewriting ambiguous sentences with an LLM — can improve downstream classification. The annotation procedure that operationalises this taxonomy is described in Chapter~3.

\section{BERT and Transformer-Based Classification}

To measure the effect of ambiguity on classification, a model is needed that has been shown to work well on this specific task. BERT \cite{devlin2019bert} — the same model family used by AI-CRAS — is such a model. It is built on the transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}, which replaced recurrent networks with a self-attention mechanism that allows every token in a sequence to attend to all others simultaneously, producing richer contextual representations than its predecessors. BERT extends this foundation by pre-training on large text corpora using masked language modelling and next-sentence prediction, yielding representations that can be fine-tuned for downstream tasks with relatively little labelled data.

Fine-tuned BERT classifiers have demonstrated strong performance across a range of requirements engineering tasks: identifying requirements from unstructured documents, classifying functional and non-functional requirements, and detecting security-related statements. AI-CRAS's results on cloud requirement classification — 0.92 F1-score on the binary task — confirm that this architecture is well-suited to the domain and the task. Using the same model family is also a deliberate methodological choice: it ensures that the results in this thesis are directly comparable to AI-CRAS's published baseline, so that any performance differences can be attributed to ambiguity rather than to a change in model.

\section{Large Language Models for Disambiguation and Classification}

If ambiguity degrades BERT's performance, the natural next question is whether it can be fixed. Large language models offer two distinct avenues for doing so.

The GPT family \cite{openai2023gpt4} introduced a paradigm shift: rather than fine-tuning on labelled data for each task, these models are prompted with natural language instructions and respond accordingly. This makes them natural candidates for requirement disambiguation — given an ambiguous sentence, an LLM can be asked to rewrite it in clearer terms while preserving the original intent, without requiring any task-specific training. This thesis tests whether feeding such rewritten sentences back to the BERT classifier (RQ2) recovers the performance lost to ambiguity.

Beyond rewriting, LLMs can also perform classification directly through prompting, bypassing BERT entirely. Brown et al.\ demonstrated that GPT-3 can perform few-shot classification by conditioning on a small number of labelled examples provided in the prompt \cite{brown2020gpt3}, and Wei et al.\ showed that instruction-tuned models generalise to unseen classification tasks in zero-shot settings \cite{wei2022flan}. Whether this general capability extends to the specific and technical domain of cloud requirement classification — and whether it is competitive with a fine-tuned BERT model — is what RQ3 investigates.
