\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}

This chapter interprets the experimental findings, answers the three research questions, discusses the limitations of the study, and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Interpretation of Results}

The central empirical finding of this thesis is a null result: none of the ambiguity manipulations altered BERT's classification performance in a measurable way. Training and evaluating on the unambiguous subset alone produced results statistically indistinguishable from the full-dataset baseline, and substituting LLM-rewritten versions of ambiguous sentences was equally neutral. At the same time, the fine-tuned BERT model consistently outperformed every large language model used directly as a classifier.

The absence of an effect of ambiguity can be read in two complementary ways, and both are supported by the data. The first is that ambiguity, as it occurs in this corpus, does not materially impede BERT-based classification. Requirements language, even when vague, remains sufficiently distinctive that the model can assign the correct service categories; BERT's contextual word representations may be robust to the qualitative, underspecified phrasing that characterises ambiguous sentences. Under this reading, the practical conclusion is reassuring: ambiguity-aware pre-processing is not necessary for accurate classification on this task.

The second reading is that the effect of ambiguity is genuinely difficult to measure because the construct itself is difficult to pin down. Chapter 3 showed that a well-calibrated, independently instructed annotator---an LLM---agreed with the manual annotation on only about 82\% of sentences (Cohen's kappa of 0.48), and flagged a substantially larger share of the corpus as ambiguous (582 \textit{vs.}~312 sentences). In other words, two reasonable annotators can disagree on over a third of the sentences about whether they are ambiguous at all. If the independent variable of a study is this unstable, any true effect it has on the dependent variable is diluted by measurement error and hard to detect with a dataset of this size. The moderate kappa was therefore not merely a methodological detail; it is a substantive finding that shapes how the experimental results should be interpreted.

A second, consistent pattern runs through the results: over-prediction. Both BERT and the LLMs assign labels liberally, achieving high recall at the cost of precision (for the BERT baseline, binary recall 0.942 against precision 0.816; for the LLMs, recall exceeded precision on every category). This is a property of the operating point---a threshold of 0.20 inherited from AI-CRAS---combined with the nature of the data: genuine requirements are usually expressed clearly enough that the models rarely miss one, while false positives concentrate on borderline, often vague, sentences. It is plausible, though not established here, that precisely these borderline sentences are the ones on which ambiguity annotation and classifier behaviour are most likely to disagree.

Finally, the reproduced BERT baseline (binary requirement F1 of 0.874, macro F1 of 0.675) falls somewhat below the figures reported for the original AI-CRAS system (0.92 and 0.76, respectively). As discussed in Section~6.3, this gap most likely reflects differences in the evaluation protocol rather than a defect in the model, and it does not affect the internal comparisons of this thesis, in which all conditions were evaluated identically.

\section{Answers to Research Questions}

\subsection{RQ1: Does ambiguity negatively affect BERT classification performance?}

No. Under the annotation used in this thesis, ambiguity has no measurable effect on BERT's classification performance. Removing the 312 sentences judged ambiguous left the macro-averaged F1 essentially unchanged (0.667 \textit{vs.}~0.675 for the full-dataset baseline), a difference well within the run-to-run variability, and the binary requirement F1 was likewise unaffected (0.871 \textit{vs.}~0.874). At the category level, no category moved by more than about 0.06 in F1, and the shifts showed no consistent direction.

Regarding the second part of the question---whether any category is particularly affected---the answer is also no. Although ambiguity rates vary substantially across categories (from 12.1\% for Security \& Compliance to 23.5\% for Cloud Service Essentials, Section~3.3), the categories with the highest ambiguity rates were not the ones whose performance changed most when ambiguous sentences were removed. This suggests that, if ambiguity matters at all, its influence is not concentrated in particular service categories but is instead diffuse across the corpus---or, as argued above, too weakly measured to surface. Chapter 3's reliability analysis indicates that the second explanation cannot be dismissed.

\subsection{RQ2: Can LLM disambiguation improve BERT classification accuracy?}

No. Replacing ambiguous sentences with their LLM-rewritten counterparts produced performance statistically indistinguishable from the baseline (macro F1 of 0.673 \textit{vs.}~0.675; binary requirement F1 of 0.871 \textit{vs.}~0.874). The category with the highest ambiguity rate, Cloud Service Essentials, improved only negligibly (0.642 to 0.651).

This result is the logical counterpart of the RQ1 finding. If ambiguity does not degrade classification in the first place, there is little room for disambiguation to improve it. It is worth emphasising that the disambiguation was not ineffective as a text transformation: the rewrites replaced vague phrasing with concrete, measurable clarifications while preserving meaning (Section~4.2). The finding is therefore that making requirements clearer does not make them easier to classify, not that the rewriting failed. This distinction is important for how the contribution of LLM-based disambiguation should be judged: it may still be valuable for human readers and downstream contract analysis, even if it does not change automated classification accuracy.

\subsection{RQ3: Can LLMs match or exceed BERT as direct classifiers?}

No. Neither LLM, in either prompting configuration, matched the fine-tuned BERT baseline. The best LLM configuration (zero-shot GPT\textsubscript{4.1-mini}) reached a binary requirement F1 of 0.856 against the BERT baseline's 0.874, and all LLM conditions fell well short on the multi-label task (macro F1 of 0.546--0.574 against 0.675).

The LLMs were nonetheless competitive on binary detection, achieving the same high recall as BERT (0.893--0.947) and a requirement F1 within three points of the fine-tuned model, entirely from a prompt. Their weakness was concentrated in the multi-label task, where per-category precision was low and the models systematically over-predicted---behaviour that few-shot demonstrations did not consistently correct. The practical conclusion is that general-purpose prompting does not currently replace fine-tuning for this domain task, but that LLMs are a viable, training-free option for high-recall requirement screening, and a useful tool for disambiguation and annotation, as Chapters 3 and 4 demonstrate.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}

\textbf{Construct validity of ambiguity.} The most important limitation concerns the definition and measurement of ambiguity itself. The annotation follows a single-pass, single-annotator protocol over a five-way taxonomy, assigning one dominant type per sentence. Chapter 3 documents that a second, independent annotation produced by an LLM agreed only moderately with the manual labels (binary kappa of 0.48), and that the two annotators differed systematically in how liberally they flagged sentences. The final labels used in this thesis embody one reasonable operationalisation of ambiguity---a sentence is ambiguous only if it admits more than one plausible interpretation---but other operationalisations would yield different datasets and could, in principle, yield different experimental results. The reliability evidence should therefore be read as an honest bound on how much can be concluded from any single annotation.

\textbf{The use of an LLM as a reliability reference.} Measuring inter-annotator agreement against an LLM is not the same as measuring it against a second human expert, and the LLM itself may deviate from human judgement in systematic ways. The agreement figures in Chapter 3 are informative, but they are not a substitute for a human multi-annotator study, and they should be interpreted accordingly.

\textbf{Internal validity.} The dataset is comparatively small (2,166 unique sentences) and drawn from a limited number of Request for Proposal documents supplemented with synthetic examples. Class imbalances within categories are substantial, and all results depend on a fixed decision threshold (0.20) and early-stopping rule inherited from the AI-CRAS protocol. Ten seeds were used to average out training stochasticity, which leaves residual uncertainty of roughly $\pm$0.03 in macro F1; effects smaller than this would not be detected, and the RQ1 and RQ2 null results are best stated as ``no effect larger than approximately 0.03 in macro F1''.

\textbf{The reproduced BERT baseline.} The BERT baseline reproduced in this thesis scores below the original AI-CRAS results (binary F1 of 0.874 \textit{vs.}~0.92). The two evaluations are not directly comparable: they differ in data preprocessing, the number and composition of the validation folds, and evaluation details. All internal comparisons in this thesis are unaffected, since every condition was evaluated under the identical protocol, but readers should be cautious about comparing the absolute figures reported here with those of AI-CRAS or of other work.

\textbf{External validity.} The experiments concern a single task---classifying requirements in cloud-computing procurement documents into a fixed six-category ontology. The findings may not transfer to other domains, other requirement ontologies, or other languages. The LLM results are also a snapshot: the models were accessed through a commercial API at a specific point in time, and while temperature was set to zero, API-side updates and residual nondeterminism mean the exact figures may not be perfectly reproducible.

\textbf{Disambiguation quality.} The deambiguified corpus was produced automatically and was not manually validated in full. Although the rewriting procedure enforces concrete clarifications and preserves the original sentences verbatim when they are judged unambiguous, imperfect rewrites cannot be excluded and would weaken the RQ2 test. Non-ambiguous sentences were kept unchanged, so this concern is limited to the rewritten subset.

\section{Future Work}

The most direct extension of this thesis is a proper multi-annotator study of ambiguity. Having several human annotators label the corpus---or a large sample of it---would provide a genuine inter-annotator agreement measure, clarify which sentences are genuinely ambiguous and which merely reflect individual judgement, and yield a higher-confidence label set. Given the moderate agreement observed with an LLM annotator, such a study is arguably a prerequisite for any stronger claim about the effect of ambiguity on classification.

Second, the robustness of the null result should be tested under alternative operationalisations of ambiguity. Refining the annotation rubric, restricting the analysis to sentences on which multiple annotators agree, or studying specific ambiguity types separately could reveal effects that the present, conservative definition washes out. The annotation infrastructure developed here makes such re-analysis straightforward.

Third, the classifier side can be extended. The comparison between a fine-tuned BERT model and prompted LLMs leaves open the middle ground of fine-tuned or instruction-tuned LLMs (for example, parameter-efficient fine-tuning), which may combine the domain adaptation of BERT with the reasoning abilities of LLMs. Evaluating such models on this task would complete the picture that RQ3 begins.

Fourth, the LLM's demonstrated ability to annotate ambiguity and to rewrite ambiguous sentences could be turned into a practical pipeline: automated ambiguity detection used to route suspicious sentences to human review, rather than to rewrite them wholesale. This would exploit the model's liberal flagging as a triage mechanism---an application that is natural given the annotation agreement analysis of Chapter 3.

Finally, the generalisability questions remain open: larger and more diverse requirement corpora, additional domains beyond cloud computing, and other classification ontologies would establish whether the findings of this thesis---in particular the null effect of ambiguity---hold beyond the specific setting studied here.

\section{Conclusion}

This thesis investigated whether ambiguity in natural-language cloud-computing requirements affects the automated classification of those requirements. It made three contributions: an ambiguity-annotated extension of the AI-CRAS dataset, together with an empirical reliability analysis of that annotation; an evaluation of how ambiguity affects BERT-based classification, including per-category breakdowns; and a comparative evaluation of large language models as both a disambiguation tool and a direct classifier.

The results are best summarised as a coherent, and partly cautionary, picture. Ambiguity annotation is a genuinely subjective exercise: an independent LLM annotator agreed only moderately with the manual labels, and disagreed on over a third of the corpus. Under the resulting annotation, ambiguity had no measurable effect on BERT's classification performance, and LLM-based rewriting of ambiguous sentences did not improve it. Finally, fine-tuned BERT clearly outperformed general-purpose LLMs used directly as classifiers, particularly on the fine-grained multi-label task.

These findings suggest two possibilities that the thesis cannot fully separate: either ambiguity matters less for automated classification than the requirements-engineering literature assumes, or it is too subjectively defined to be measured reliably at the scale of this study. Both readings carry practical value. For practitioners, they indicate that fine-tuned models are robust to the vagueness that pervades real requirements documents, and that LLM-based disambiguation, while useful for human-facing purposes, is not a prerequisite for accurate classification. For researchers, they underline that the operationalisation of ambiguity---who labels it, under what definition---deserves as much attention as the classifier it is used to study.
